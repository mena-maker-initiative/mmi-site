01ResourceRegional Pipeline Index

Where the ISEF pathway actually exists in your country

Researched directly, not assumed. Each entry below is tagged by how confident we are — confirmed pathways we found current evidence for, ones that need a direct check because our sources were dated, and real gaps where we found nothing. Society for Science's own Find-a-Fair directory is always the final word.

Egypt Confirmed

ISEF Egypt (run by the Arab Science & Technology Foundation) is the official Society-affiliated pathway. EISTF is a second, separate Cairo-based fair also open nationwide — worth entering both.

Saudi Arabia Confirmed

The Mawhiba Science & Engineering Fair, run by the King Abdulaziz & His Companions Foundation for Giftedness and Creativity, sent finalists to Regeneron ISEF 2026 — an active, current pathway.

Jordan Confirmed

The Science Fair of the Jordanian Ministry of Education sent finalists to Regeneron ISEF 2026. A separate national fair supported by the Abdul Hameed Shoman Foundation has also historically fed into ISEF.

United Arab Emirates Verify Directly

The Think Science program (Emirates Foundation) has historically been a pathway to ISEF, but our most current confirmation of it is several years old. Dubai also runs its own science fair (DSEF) — affiliation status unconfirmed. Check Find-a-Fair before relying on either.

Bahrain No Fair Found

We could not confirm a Society-affiliated fair based in Bahrain. If you're based there, go directly to Find-a-Fair on societyforscience.org — affiliations open and change, and this is a real gap in the regional pipeline.

Kuwait, Lebanon, Morocco, Tunisia, Oman, Qatar Not Yet Researched

We did not find current, reliable evidence of active pathways for these countries in this pass. That doesn't mean nothing exists — it means check Find-a-Fair directly rather than trust an absence on this list.

02GuideUniversity Admissions 101

How applications actually work, system by system

Almost every application system worldwide falls into two philosophies. Knowing which one a target university uses changes what you should actually spend your time on.

Stats-Based Systems

UK (UCAS), most of Europe, and most direct-apply systems weigh grades and predicted grades heavily, with a single personal statement and sometimes an admissions test or interview. Activities matter far less than most students assume.

Holistic Systems

US (Common App) universities weigh grades, tests, essays, recommendation letters, and extracurricular depth together — no single factor dominates, which is why the same student can be rejected by one school and admitted to a "harder" one.

Direct-Apply Systems

Many Gulf, Asian, and some European universities admit directly off grades/test scores with minimal or no essay component — check each target university's own portal rather than assuming a UCAS/Common App-style process applies.

SystemUsed ByWhat Matters Most
UCASUK universitiesPredicted grades, one personal statement (shared across all course choices), sometimes an admissions test
Common AppMost US universitiesGrades, essays (main + supplements per school), recommendation letters, activities, sometimes test scores
Direct-apply portalsVaries widely — check each universityUsually grades/test scores first; requirements vary enough that no single rule applies

The practical takeaway: figure out which system(s) your target universities actually use before deciding where to invest your time — a brilliant Common App essay is wasted effort if every school on your list uses UCAS.

03GuideStandardized Testing — The SAT

What it actually is, and how to prep for free

The SAT moved fully digital in recent years — the format below reflects the current Digital SAT, not the old paper version many older guides still describe.

Format

Two sections — Reading & Writing (64 min, two 32-min modules) and Math (70 min, two 35-min modules). About 2h24m total, including breaks.

Scoring

400–1600 total, two section scores of 200–800 each. The second module in each section adapts in difficulty based on your first-module performance.

Who Actually Needs It

Mainly relevant for US-bound applicants — many UK and direct-apply universities don't require it. Check each target university before assuming you need it.

Free prep that's actually good enough

The College Board's official partnership with Khan Academy means free prep is no longer the weaker option it used to be — a consistent student can reasonably reach a strong score without paying for anything.

ResourceWhat It's ForLink
Bluebook (College Board's official app)Full-length, official adaptive practice tests — the closest simulation of the real examcollegeboard.org/digital/bluebook
Official Digital SAT Prep on Khan AcademyFree, personalized skill practice aligned directly with College Board contentkhanacademy.org/sat

A realistic plan: link your Khan Academy account to College Board, do a diagnostic Bluebook test first to find weak areas, then alternate targeted Khan Academy practice with 3–5 full Bluebook practice tests in the month before your real test date. Register early — international test centers (relevant across Bahrain, Egypt, and the wider region) fill up and aren't available everywhere.

04GuideInternational Curricula — A-Levels, IB & AP

How each one actually gets used in applications

A-Levels

Typically 3–4 subjects. UCAS applications run on predicted grades submitted before final exams — offers are usually conditional on meeting them in the real results.

IB Diploma

Scored out of 45. Predicted scores are used the same way A-Level predictions are — submitted before finals, with offers conditional on meeting them.

AP

Individual subject exams (1–5 each), not a full diploma on their own — usually paired with another curriculum. Signals subject strength and sometimes earns college credit directly.

The predicted-grades trap: predicted grades are set by your school, not you — start the conversation with your teachers early about what they're likely to predict, since it directly shapes which universities are realistic to apply to.

05GuideNational Curricula — Thanaweya Amma, Tawjihi & Others

Thanaweya Amma, Tawjihi, and international applications

Most guidance online is written for A-Level, IB, or AP students by default. If you're on a national curriculum, here's what's actually different — and what isn't.

What's needed

UK (UCAS) and US (Common App) systems both accept Thanaweya Amma and Tawjihi transcripts directly — the standard requirement is a certified, word-for-word English translation of your official transcript, not a separate equivalency exam.

The real timing problem

Thanaweya Amma results are released very late in the year relative to when UK/US application decisions are being made — plan around this explicitly with your school counselor rather than discovering it in application season.

Same deadlines apply

UCAS and Common App deadlines don't shift for national-curriculum students — you're on the exact same calendar as A-Level/IB applicants (see the Timeline section), just with a different transcript format.

Start the translation early. Certified translation can take real time to arrange through your school or a certified translator — this is the one step that catches national-curriculum students off guard most often, purely because it's a logistics problem, not an academic one. The same logic applies to Lebanese Baccalaureate, Indian CBSE/ISC, and most other national curricula — a certified translation, not a conversion exam, is usually the actual requirement for applying abroad.

06GuideScholarships Strategy

Finding funding is a separate process from getting admitted

Admission and funding are often decided by completely different processes, sometimes with different deadlines — treating them as one step is the most common scholarship mistake.

Merit-Based

Awarded for academic/talent achievement, often through the university itself or external competitions — usually needs no separate financial paperwork.

Need-Based

Awarded based on financial circumstances, typically requiring separate financial documentation (e.g., CSS Profile for many US universities) — international aid varies hugely by school.

External/Private

Awarded by foundations, governments, or organizations independent of any university — often the most overlooked source since they require their own separate search.

Where to actually find listings: the Opportunity Database is where real scholarship and university funding entries live as they get researched and verified — this section covers strategy, not a list, to avoid duplicating (and risking contradicting) that page.

Two practical rules: scholarship deadlines are sometimes earlier than the admission deadline itself — never assume they're the same. And admission does not imply funding — "accepted" and "funded" are two separate decisions at most universities.

07GuideBuilding Your Portfolio

Depth on a few things beats breadth on many

A portfolio is not a resume with extra steps — it's evidence. Every Vault project you complete is a natural building block for one.

01

Document while you build, not after

Photos of your BP-001 build mid-assembly, your RS-002 field data as you collect it, your MO-001 problem-solving process — evidence collected in the moment is far more convincing than a reconstruction written weeks later.

02

Pick 2–3 projects to go deep on

A portfolio listing ten shallow things reads worse than one showing three things you can talk about for twenty minutes each. Depth is the signal admissions readers are actually looking for.

03

Put code and write-ups somewhere permanent

A GitHub repository for any coding/data work, and a short write-up (problem, approach, result, what you'd do differently) for every project — Section 12 covers writing these well.

08GuideResearch & Abstracts — The Project Blueprint

Turning a hardware build into an ISEF-compliant submission

A finished blueprint from the Vault is a working build, not yet a research project. Here's exactly what ISEF requires, sourced from the official rules — and the actual steps to get from "I built a sensor array" to a submission that meets every one of them.

250
Word Abstract Limit

One page, on the official ISEF abstract form, describing only the student's own work.

240×122×76
Max Board Size (cm)

Height × width × depth — confirm against your specific affiliated fair, as some vary slightly from the ISEF standard.

12
Months Max Research

Continuous research window — can't combine work from multiple unrelated years into one submission.

Also required: Checklist Form 1, Student Checklist Form 1A, and the Research Plan & Approval Form 1B for every project — plus Form 1C if any work happened somewhere other than home, school, or field. Every graphic on your board (photos, charts, clip art) needs individual citation — a blanket "all graphics by student" statement is no longer accepted. All materials must be in English.

From finished build to research submission

01

Find the real question inside the build

A blueprint proves a technique works. A research project asks something the technique can answer. The question usually appears the moment you ask "what could I measure with this, that I don't already know the answer to?"

BP-001's sensor array → "Does greenhouse humidity variance predict next-day plant stress better than temperature alone?"
02

Define your variables before you build further

What are you changing (independent variable), what are you measuring (dependent variable), and what are you holding constant (controls)? Write these down before collecting data — judges specifically look for this.

03

Collect data over real time, not one afternoon

This is exactly what BP-002's SD logging and RTC timestamps are for — a single reading is an anecdote, a logged dataset over days or weeks is evidence.

04

Write the abstract last, not first

Problem statement (1–2 sentences), methodology (2–3 sentences), key results (2–3 sentences), conclusion (1–2 sentences). All of it under 250 words, describing what you actually found — not what you hoped to find.

05

Design the board around your data, not your build

Judges spend more time on your results section than your hardware photos. Lead with the graph or finding that answers your question; the build itself is supporting evidence, not the headline.

09GuideOlympiads

Why Olympiads matter, and how to start

Olympiad results are one of the clearest, most internationally legible signals in an application — a math or programming Olympiad medal means the same thing to an admissions reader in London as it does in Cairo.

01

Start local, not international

School and national-level competitions build the specific skills (proof-writing, competitive-programming speed) that international Olympiads assume you already have. Skipping straight to IMO-level prep without this foundation usually backfires.

02

Pick a track and commit

Math and programming Olympiads reward sustained multi-year preparation far more than general talent — the students who medal almost always started 2–3 years before their result.

03

Use the Vault to get started

MO-001 (Getting Started with Olympiad Mathematics) and PO-001 (Getting Started with Competitive Programming) in the Vault cover choosing your specific competition and building a realistic training plan.

10GuideInternships & Cold Emailing

Getting a "yes" from someone who doesn't know you yet

Most valuable under-18 research and industry opportunities aren't advertised — they come from someone saying yes to a cold email. The skill is learnable.

Keep it short

A cold email a professor or professional will actually read is 5–7 sentences, not a full cover letter — get to the specific ask fast.

Be specific

"I'd love to learn from you" gets ignored. "I read your paper on X and had a specific question about Y" gets a reply.

Expect a low response rate

Most emails won't get a reply — that's normal, not a sign you did it wrong. Volume and specificity matter more than any single email being perfect.

Follow up once, politely, about a week after the first email if you haven't heard back — then move on. A single polite follow-up is expected; more than that reads as pressure, not persistence.

11GuideRecommendation Letters

Who to ask, and how to make their letter easy to write well

Who to ask

A teacher who knows your actual work well beats a more senior name who barely knows you — specificity in a letter is what makes it strong.

What to give them

A short document with your key projects, achievements, and what you'd want highlighted — recommenders write stronger letters when they don't have to reconstruct your work from memory.

When to ask

At least 4–6 weeks before the deadline — great recommenders are often asked by multiple students, and rushed letters read as rushed.

12GuideEssays & Personal Statements

Structure and voice, not templates

UCAS Personal Statement

One essay shared across all course choices — focus on genuine subject interest and fit, not a life story unrelated to the course.

Common App Essay

One main personal essay plus supplemental essays per school — the main essay is narrative and personal; supplements should answer the specific school's actual question.

The most common mistake

Writing what you think admissions readers want to hear, rather than what's actually true and specific to you — generic "passion" language is instantly recognizable and forgettable.

Start from a real moment, not a theme. Essays built around one specific, concrete moment (a failed experiment, a real conversation) consistently read stronger than essays built around an abstract quality you're trying to demonstrate.

13GuideInterviews

Know your own application better than anyone else in the room

01

Re-read your own application first

Interviewers frequently ask directly about something you wrote — being unable to elaborate on your own essay is a bad, avoidable moment.

02

Practice explaining your projects simply

Academic (Oxbridge-style) interviews test how you think through a problem live, not just what you already know — practice thinking out loud, not just reciting facts.

03

Prepare real questions to ask them

A generic "no questions" moment at the end is a missed opportunity — one specific, well-researched question shows genuine interest better than anything else in the interview.

14GuideApplication Timeline

Fitting all of this around exams, not instead of them

A template to adapt, not a fixed calendar — every school's exam schedule differs. The point is the shape: start early, keep research lightweight in the background, and never let anything above collide with your actual exams.

Term 1

Foundation & early moves

  • Work through a Vault blueprint end to end — this is where your technical foundation and portfolio evidence come from
  • Identify your real research question (Section 08) and start the recommendation-letter conversation (Section 11) early
  • Check the Regional Pipeline Index and Opportunity Database for registration windows — most open in autumn
  • UCAS Oxbridge/Medicine/Dentistry/Veterinary deadline: 15 October
Term 2

Applications open, research stays lightweight

  • Common App Early Decision/Action: ~1–15 November. Common App Regular Decision and UCAS main deadline: January
  • Let a logging build (like BP-002) run in the background collecting data — this should cost minutes a week, not hours
  • Do not schedule new research work during exam weeks — a data logger running unattended is fine; starting new experiments is not
  • Draft your abstract and essays early, even roughly — tightening a bad draft is far easier than writing one from nothing under deadline pressure
Term 3 / Spring

Fair season & decisions

  • Local and national affiliated fairs generally run January–April — confirm your specific fair's date early, don't assume
  • Finalize your board and abstract at least two weeks before your fair, not the night before
  • If you advance, Regeneron ISEF itself is typically in May — hold that week deliberately open
  • University decisions typically arrive through spring — remember admission and funding (Section 06) can be separate decisions

The one rule that matters most: if a research deadline and an exam ever genuinely conflict, the exam wins. A missed fair registration is recoverable next cycle. A damaged transcript is not. Build everything around your academic calendar — never the other way around.
